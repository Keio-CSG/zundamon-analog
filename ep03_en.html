3Why use both large-signal and small-signal analysis?

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Can't circuit theory solve the original curve?

M

Shikoku Metan

Superposition, Thevenin's theorem, and Laplace methods assume linear circuits.

M

Shikoku Metan

A MOSFET is nonlinear: doubling VGS doesn't simply double ID.

Shikoku Metan: So use two steps. Large-signal analysis first finds the nonlinear DC bias point.

M

Shikoku Metan

Then small-signal analysis solves only changes in the circuit linearized there.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

I don't get it. Why solve the same circuit twice?

M

Shikoku Metan

Numerically, you can solve it all at once. Splitting it reveals gain formulas.

Shikoku Metan: The total is DC plus the small signal, within the range where linearization works.

4Defining gm: the tangent slope

M

Shikoku Metan

Back to the tangent. Its slope is called transconductance, gm.

Shikoku Metan: gm = ∂ID/∂VGS, differentiating while VDS stays fixed.

M

Shikoku Metan

So id=gm·vgs: a voltage change produces a current change.

Shikoku Metan: The unit is siemens, S. One mA/V equals one mS.

5gm = K·Vov = 2ID/Vov in saturation

M

Shikoku Metan

In saturation, ID = ½K·Vov². Ignore channel-length modulation for now.

Shikoku Metan: Differentiate with respect to VGS: gm = K·Vov, proportional to overdrive.

M

Shikoku Metan

Using ID, gm = 2ID/Vov, or √(2K·ID).

M

Shikoku Metan

Use the bias-point values of ID and Vov in these formulas.

6Numerical example and the small-signal condition

M

Shikoku Metan

In this model, the bias-point current is about 0.18 mA.

Shikoku Metan: Vov is 0.4 V, so 2ID/Vov gives about 0.9 mA/V.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

But K·Vov gives 0.8 mA/V. Why don't they match?

M

Shikoku Metan

The extra ten percent is (1 + λVDS). The 2ID/Vov form includes it.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

How small does a small signal need to be?

M

Shikoku Metan

A 10 mV input change gives about 9 µA, almost exactly gm·vgs.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

What about a 100 mV change?

M

Shikoku Metan

Upward gives about +0.10 mA; downward about −0.08 mA. They're unequal.

Shikoku Metan: vgs must be much smaller than twice Vov for the small-signal approximation.

7The NMOS small-signal equivalent circuit: four steps

M

Shikoku Metan

Let's build the NMOS small-signal equivalent, one step at a time.

M

Shikoku Metan

Step one: separate the DC bias from the small signal.

Shikoku Metan: A DC voltage source has no signal voltage, so replace it with a short to ground.

Shikoku Metan: A DC current source has no signal current, so replace it with an open circuit.

Z

Zundamon

Zero change! Zero voltage is a short, zero current is an open!

Zundamon: Wait! Won't removing the supply turn the transistor off?

M

Shikoku Metan

Only in the small-signal circuit. Large-signal analysis already found the bias.

M

Shikoku Metan

Its effects remain in gm and ro. The supply's role hasn't disappeared.

Shikoku Metan: A resistor has the same R for changes in voltage and current, so keep it.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

What about a capacitor?

M

Shikoku Metan

Open for DC. For a small signal, its impedance is 1/(jωC).

Shikoku Metan: Large capacitance or high frequency lowers it. Small enough, it acts as a short.

Shikoku Metan: Step two: keep the same three terminals, gate, drain, and source.

M

Shikoku Metan

No current flows into the gate, so gate to source is open.

Shikoku Metan: vgs is the gate-source signal voltage, positive at the gate.

Shikoku Metan: Step three: add a current source gm·vgs directed from drain to source.

Z

Zundamon

That's the dependent source from episode one!

M

Shikoku Metan

Step four: add ro in parallel to represent channel-length modulation.

Shikoku Metan: As last time, at VGS=0.8 V, ro is about 62 kΩ.

M

Shikoku Metan

Together, id=gm·vgs+vds/ro.

Shikoku Metan: The body is tied to the source, so the body-effect contribution is zero.

Shikoku Metan: We're also omitting MOSFET capacitances for this low-frequency model.

Shikoku Metan: In the test circuit, short the VDS supply. Then vds=0 and id=gm·vgs.

Shikoku Metan: Here's the replacement table. Each model uses the slope at the bias point.

8Intrinsic gain gm·ro

M

Shikoku Metan

gm·ro is intrinsic gain: a guide to the gain available from one transistor.

M

Shikoku Metan

Here it's about 56. The approximation 2/(λVov) gives about 50.

9Common-source amplifier: load line and transfer curve

M

Shikoku Metan

Now the common-source amplifier: RD and VDD at the drain, source grounded.

M

Shikoku Metan

Use VDD=1.8 V and RD=5 kΩ, with VIN at the gate.

Shikoku Metan: The drain voltage is the output: VOUT=VDD−RD·ID.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

More ID means a bigger drop across RD and a lower output?

M

Shikoku Metan

Right. But ID also depends on VOUT, so solve both equations together.

M

Shikoku Metan

The current equation is nonlinear. Solving it numerically is large-signal analysis.

Shikoku Metan: On this graph, find the intersection with RD's straight line, the load line.

Z

Zundamon

Raise VIN, and the curve rises while the intersection moves left!

M

Shikoku Metan

Find VOUT for each VIN and join the points to get this transfer curve.

M

Shikoku Metan

Below Vth, current is tiny, so VOUT stays close to VDD.

Shikoku Metan: Above Vth, the device enters saturation and VOUT drops sharply.

Shikoku Metan: Near VIN=0.9 V and VOUT=0.5 V, it crosses into the linear region.

Shikoku Metan: Bias at VIN=0.8 V along that steep part: VOUT is about 0.92 V.

M

Shikoku Metan

The slope here is the small-signal voltage gain, Av.

10Small-signal circuit and Av = −gm(RD∥ro)

M

Shikoku Metan

Now small-signal analysis. Transform the circuit to derive that gain.

Shikoku Metan: Step one: VDD is a DC voltage source, so short it to signal ground.

M

Shikoku Metan

Short the input's 0.8 V DC component too. Only vin remains.

Z

Zundamon

So the top of RD connects to ground!

M

Shikoku Metan

Step two: replace the NMOS with its small-signal model.

M

Shikoku Metan

The gate has vin and the source is grounded, so vgs=vin.

Shikoku Metan: Step three: both ro and RD connect between output and ground.

Z

Zundamon

The same two nodes! They're in parallel!

M

Shikoku Metan

Redraw it as a gm·vin current source with RD∥ro.

Shikoku Metan: The source draws current from the output: vout = −gm·vin·(RD∥ro).

M

Shikoku Metan

So Av = −gm(RD∥ro). The minus sign means the output moves opposite to the input.

Shikoku Metan: Here gm≈0.87 mA/V and RD∥ro≈4.6 kΩ, so Av ≈ −4.

Z

Zundamon

It matches the graph's slope!

M

Shikoku Metan

A 10 mV input gives an inverted output wave of about 40 mV.

11Output swing and what the two analyses tell us

M

Shikoku Metan

Swing is limited: a +100 mV input reaches the boundary near a 0.5 V output.

M

Shikoku Metan

The transistor must remain in saturation throughout the signal swing.

13Solving small-signal circuits efficiently: Rin, Rout, and Gm

M

Shikoku Metan

Let's practice some shortcuts for solving small-signal circuits.

M

Shikoku Metan

Input resistance Rin looks into the input. At a MOS gate, it's infinite here.

Shikoku Metan: With input zero, Rout looks into the output: RD∥ro, about 4.6 kΩ.

M

Shikoku Metan

Apply a test voltage vx and find incoming current ix. Resistance is vx/ix.

M

Shikoku Metan

Think of gain as Av = −Gm·Rout: voltage-to-current conversion times resistance.

Z

Zundamon

For common source, Gm=gm, so we immediately get −gm(RD∥ro)!

M

Shikoku Metan

Practice with a MOSFET whose gate and drain are tied: a diode connection.

M

Shikoku Metan

vgs=vx, so ix=gm·vx+vx/ro. Its resistance is (1/gm)∥ro.

M

Shikoku Metan

ro is much larger, so resistance is about 1/gm. We'll use this in episode four.

Shikoku Metan: Next, add RS=1 kΩ between the source and ground.

M

Shikoku Metan

For DC, raise the gate to 1.0 V. VOUT≈0.88 V, still in saturation.

M

Shikoku Metan

The signal source voltage is id·RS, so vgs=vin−id·RS.

Shikoku Metan: Ignoring ro, Gm=gm/(1+gm·RS), smaller than gm.

M

Shikoku Metan

Av ≈ −RD/(RS+1/gm) ≈ −2.3. The model's slope is about −2.2.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

The gain dropped. What's the advantage?

M

Shikoku Metan

A ten percent gm change changes gain only about five percent, reducing distortion.

Shikoku Metan: 1/gm is the resistance looking into the source. More in episode five.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

What if a large capacitor is connected across RS?

M

Shikoku Metan

For the signal it shorts RS, restoring gain to about −4. That's a bypass capacitor.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

If the signal source has zero DC voltage, how do we keep the gate at 0.8 V?

M

Shikoku Metan

Add a coupling capacitor and bias the gate through a large resistor RG.

Shikoku Metan: For DC, the capacitor is open. No gate current flows, so the gate stays at 0.8 V.

M

Shikoku Metan

For signals, short the cap and DC source. Rin becomes RG=100 kΩ.
