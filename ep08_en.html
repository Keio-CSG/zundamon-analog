Episode 8

Op-Amps: Two Rules for Amplifiers and Integrators

Build a ten-times amplifier with an op-amp. Learn negative feedback and the two ideal rules, then derive the follower, noninverting amplifier, inverting amplifier, and integrator. Look inside a CMOS op-amp to see how finite gain, loading, bandwidth, output swing, and offset limit real circuits.

1Introduction: build a ten-times amplifier

Z

Zundamon

My lab assignment's in trouble, nanoda! "Build a gain-of-10 amplifier with an op-amp."

M

Shikoku Metan

The op-amp is the most-used part in analog circuits. Let's start with how to use it.

Z

Zundamon

I've heard of op-amps, nanoda! They're in all kinds of circuits!

M

Shikoku Metan

Yes. Sensor amplifiers, audio, power control. They're used all over the place.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

But I only know the triangle symbol. Can I use one, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

Sure. First half: no need to know the inside. Second half: CMOS internals and limits.

M

Shikoku Metan

The symbol is this triangle. Two inputs, plus and minus, one output, and power terminals.

M

Shikoku Metan

Schematics often omit the supply, but the output's power comes from it.

M

Shikoku Metan

In the first half, assume a supply that lets the output swing high or low freely.

M

Shikoku Metan

It does one thing: it amplifies the voltage difference between the plus and minus inputs.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Only the difference, nanoda? What if both inputs rise by 1 V?

M

Shikoku Metan

The difference doesn't change, so ideally neither does the output.

M

Shikoku Metan

As an equation: vout = A(v+ − v−). A is called the open-loop gain.

M

Shikoku Metan

This A is huge. General-purpose parts often exceed 100,000.

Z

Zundamon

With 100,000, a gain of 10 is a piece of cake, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

Even a 1 mV input difference would calculate to 100 V out, above the supply voltage.

M

Shikoku Metan

In reality, the output sticks near the supply. This is called saturation.

M

Shikoku Metan

As is, it can only tell which input is higher.

Z

Zundamon

So all it can do is amplify by 100,000? That's hard to use, nanoda...

Zundamon: Then why is such a hard-to-use part used everywhere, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

Good question. External resistors and capacitors let you set its function freely.

M

Shikoku Metan

Unit 1 recap: an op-amp hugely amplifies the input difference. Alone, it saturates.

M

Shikoku Metan

The trick is to send the output back to the input: feedback.

Z

Zundamon

I know feedback, nanoda! Look at the result and use it next time.

M

Shikoku Metan

Feeding back to cancel the error is negative feedback: output to the − input.

M

Shikoku Metan

Like testing shower water with your hand and adding cold if it's hot. Same idea.

Z

Zundamon

Check the temperature, adjust to fix the error, nanoda! I do that every morning!

M

Shikoku Metan

Let's try the simplest form. 1 V at the + input. Output wired straight to the − input.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Won't feeding the output back go round and round and run wild, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

Fed to the − input, it works to cancel the error. Let's follow it step by step.

Shikoku Metan: If the output is below 1 V, the difference v+ − v− is positive, so the output rises.

M

Shikoku Metan

If it overshoots above 1 V, the difference goes negative, and the output falls.

Z

Zundamon

Too high or too low, it gets pushed back, and settles at 1 V, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

At that point the difference is only about 10 µV. Times 100,000, that's about 1 V.

M

Shikoku Metan

Now consider an ideal op-amp: gain A is infinite, input impedance is infinite too.

M

Shikoku Metan

The output is finite, so the input difference is vout/A. With A infinite, it's 0.

M

Shikoku Metan

With negative feedback, the + and − voltages are equal. That's rule one, the voltage rule.

M

Shikoku Metan

This state is called a virtual short.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

A short means joining two points with a wire, nanoda? Are the inputs connected?

M

Shikoku Metan

The voltages are equal, as if wired together. But no current flows between the two.

M

Shikoku Metan

Instead of a wire, the output moves the − input to match: a "virtual" short.

Z

Zundamon

Not a real wire, just an apparent short made by negative feedback, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

This assumes stable feedback, with input and output within the usable voltage range.

M

Shikoku Metan

Real A is finite, but if A is large and feedback strong, it holds. We'll check later.

M

Shikoku Metan

Rule two, the current rule: input impedance is infinite, so no current enters the inputs.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Is it negative feedback that keeps current out of the input too, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

No, that's a property of the input impedance. It holds with or without feedback.

M

Shikoku Metan

Unit 2 recap: with feedback, + and − voltages are equal and input current is 0.

Z

Zundamon

Only two rules, nanoda! I want to solve circuits with them!

M

Shikoku Metan

Now, four common circuits: follower, non-inverting, inverting, and integrator.

M

Shikoku Metan

Same method: the virtual short fixes the − input; then write KCL, input current 0.

Z

Zundamon

If one procedure solves them all, there's less to memorize, nanoda!

2What is an op-amp? Amplifying the input difference

M

Shikoku Metan

First is the circuit we just saw. It's called a voltage follower.

Shikoku Metan: Let's derive it. The − input is wired to the output, so v− = vout.

M

Shikoku Metan

By the voltage rule, v− = v+ = vin. So vout=vin, and the gain is 1.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

If it just outputs the same voltage, what's the point of connecting it, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

A 1 kΩ load wired straight to a 10 kΩ sensor drops the voltage to 1/11.

M

Shikoku Metan

With a follower in between, the current rule means it draws no current from the sensor.

M

Shikoku Metan

And ideally output impedance is 0, so load current doesn't change the output.

Z

Zundamon

Same voltage, and the op-amp does the heavy lifting, nanoda! That's a buffer!

3Negative feedback and the virtual short: two rules

M

Shikoku Metan

Second, non-inverting. Input to +; Rf and Rg divide the output back to the − input.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

You shrink the output you worked for and feed it back, nanoda? That amplifies?

M

Shikoku Metan

Step by step. No current enters the − input, so Rf and Rg carry the same current.

Shikoku Metan: So they're just in series. The divider gives v− = vout × Rg/(Rf + Rg).

M

Shikoku Metan

Next, the voltage rule: v− = vin. So vin=vout × Rg/(Rf + Rg).

M

Shikoku Metan

Rearranging: vout/vin = (Rf + Rg)/Rg = 1 + Rf/Rg. That's the gain.

Z

Zundamon

With Rg = 10 kΩ and Rf = 90 kΩ, it's 10 times, nanoda! 0.1 V becomes 1 V!

M

Shikoku Metan

One tenth of the output is fed back, so the output is 10 times the input to match.

M

Shikoku Metan

Even if A varies, gain is set mostly by the resistor ratio. That's feedback's strength.

4Voltage followers and noninverting amplifiers

M

Shikoku Metan

Third, inverting. Ground the + input; Rin and feedback resistor Rf go to the − input.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

This time the − input isn't grounded, nanoda. How is its voltage decided?

M

Shikoku Metan

By the virtual short, the − input is 0 V like the + input. Not grounded: virtual ground.

M

Shikoku Metan

It's the virtual short when the + input is grounded. Hence the name.

M

Shikoku Metan

Rin has vin and 0 V across it. By Ohm's law, the current through Rin is i = vin/Rin.

M

Shikoku Metan

KCL at the − input. Current can't enter it, so the same i flows through Rf.

M

Shikoku Metan

Rf has 0 V and vout across it. With i flowing left to right, vout=0 − Rf × i.

M

Shikoku Metan

Substituting i: vout = −(Rf/Rin)vin. The gain is −Rf/Rin.

Z

Zundamon

With Rin=10 kΩ, Rf = 100 kΩ, 0.1 V in: 10 µA flows, output −1 V, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

Opposite direction: −10 times. When the input rises, the output falls.

M

Shikoku Metan

This circuit draws current from the input through Rin, unlike the non-inverting one.

5Inverting amplifiers and integrators

M

Shikoku Metan

Fourth, the integrator. Keep input R of the inverting amp; replace Rf with a capacitor C.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Can a capacitor really integrate, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

As before, the − input is 0 V. The current through R is i = vin/R, all flowing into C.

M

Shikoku Metan

A capacitor's current is proportional to its voltage's rate of change: i = C × dv/dt.

Shikoku Metan: Current flows from the − input side to the output. Take C's voltage that way: 0−vout.

M

Shikoku Metan

So i = C × d(0−vout)/dt. That is, vin/R = −C × dvout/dt.

M

Shikoku Metan

Integrate both sides. Starting the output at 0 V: vout = −(1/RC)∫vin dt.

Z

Zundamon

The current piles up as charge in C. That's why it integrates, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

With RC = 1 ms and a +0.1 V input, the output falls 0.1 V every millisecond.

Z

Zundamon

A square wave, + and − of equal size and time, becomes a triangle wave, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

In frequency terms, replace Rf with the impedance of C, 1/(jωC).

M

Shikoku Metan

Gain is −1/(jωRC). Magnitude falls with frequency: 10× the frequency, 1/10 the output.

M

Shikoku Metan

Used in filters, waveform generators, control circuits, and many other places.

M

Shikoku Metan

Unit 3 recap: follower is 1, non-inverting is 1 + Rf/Rg, inverting is −Rf/Rin.

M

Shikoku Metan

Integrator: −(1/RC)∫vin dt. In each, the external parts set the function.

Z

Zundamon

Fix voltage and current with two rules, then solve with resistor and capacitor equations!

Z

ZundamonQuestion

But what's inside the triangle, nanoda? Is the gain really 100,000?

M

Shikoku Metan

Let's build an IC op-amp step by step, using parts from the earlier episodes.

M

Shikoku Metan

From here, a different model: ±0.9 V supply, illustrative values for about 180 nm CMOS.

6Inside CMOS: from a differential pair to two stages

M

Shikoku Metan

First, recall episode 7's differential pair with a PMOS current mirror load.

M

Shikoku Metan

Two inputs, one output, amplifying the difference. That alone is already an op-amp.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Wow, episode 7's circuit is the inside of the triangle, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

Yes. Gain magnitude is gm(ro∥ro), about 75 in this example.

M

Shikoku Metan

Let's make a non-inverting gain of 10. Treat vin and vout as small changes from the bias.

M

Shikoku Metan

With finite A: vout = A(vin−vout/10). Solving gives gain A/(1 + A/10).

M

Shikoku Metan

If A is far above 10, it's nearly 10. That's the assumption behind the voltage rule.

M

Shikoku Metan

Unlike the ideal 0, this stage's output resistance is about 54 kΩ, loaded by 100 kΩ.

M

Shikoku Metan

Gain with the load is about 49. So the intended 10 becomes only about 8.3.

Z

Zundamon

8.3 times, nanoda?! That's nowhere near the assignment's 10!

M

Shikoku Metan

One stage's gain is set by gm times ro, so there's a limit to how large it gets.

7Bandwidth, output swing, and offset limits

M

Shikoku Metan

So add another stage: episode 4's common-source amp with a current source load.

M

Shikoku Metan

The second stage inverts, so the + and − terminals swap from the one-stage case.

M

Shikoku Metan

The second stage's gain magnitude is about 47. Multiplied, the total is about 3500.

M

Shikoku Metan

The second stage's output resistance is small, about 13 kΩ. Loaded, the total is near 3100.

M

Shikoku Metan

Then the gain-of-10 non-inverting amplifier gives about 9.97. Nearly on target.

Z

Zundamon

From 8.3 to 9.97! Adding a stage brought it right up toward 10, nanoda!

Z

Zundamon

Still, it doesn't reach a general-purpose part's 100,000.

M

Shikoku Metan

Not in this simple form. Products tune the transistors and circuit to go much higher.

M

Shikoku Metan

A capacitor Cc between stage 2's input and output keeps it stable. Details later.

M

Shikoku Metan

The input is a MOSFET gate. Almost no DC current flows, so the current rule holds well.

M

Shikoku Metan

But for fast-changing signals, a little current flows into the gate capacitance.

Shikoku Metan: Unit 4 recap: one differential stage is an op-amp but lacks gain. Two stages multiply it.

M

Shikoku Metan

Besides gain, there are other internal limits. Let's see how they affect the circuits.

M

Shikoku Metan

Next, frequency. The internal gain A falls as frequency rises.

Z

ZundamonQuestion

Huh? Isn't it always 3500, nanoda?

M

Shikoku Metan

Here, we model A's frequency response with a single pole.

M

Shikoku Metan

Because of Cc, A falls from about 31 kHz; well above that, it's proportional to 1/f.

M

Shikoku Metan

Where A reaches 1, this approximation gives the gain-bandwidth product GBW: about 110 MHz.

M

Shikoku Metan

Here, GBW is estimated from the first stage's gm and Cc as gm/(2πCc).

M

Shikoku Metan

Bandwidth is where gain drops 3 dB, to about 70% of its low-frequency value.

M

Shikoku Metan

For the gain-of-10 non-inverting amp, bandwidth is about GBW/10: about 11 MHz.

Z

Zundamon

At a gain of 100, bandwidth is about 1.1 MHz. Gain and bandwidth trade off, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

The output range has a guideline: stage 2 acts as a current source to about ±0.7 V.

M

Shikoku Metan

Beyond that the output still moves a little, but the gain drops sharply.

Z

Zundamon

0.1 V times 10 is 1 V. With this inside, it can't cleanly do 10 times, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

Last, offset voltage. Even with both inputs at the same voltage, the output is shifted.

M

Shikoku Metan

Tiny differences between devices act like a small battery at the input.

M

Shikoku Metan

Here, we'll put this input-referred shift at 1 mV on the + input side.

M

Shikoku Metan

Even with the integrator's input at 0 V, the − input is held near 1 mV, not 0 V.

M

Shikoku Metan

So 0.1 µA keeps flowing through R. It passes through C, so the output keeps rising.

M

Shikoku Metan

With RC = 1 ms, the output rises about 1 V per second, past the range guideline in a second.

Z

Zundamon

The input is 0, yet the output runs off to the edge and sticks, nanoda!

M

Shikoku Metan

The fix is a large resistor in parallel with C. That makes the DC gain finite.

M

Shikoku Metan

With a resistor 100 times R, the output drift stops at about 0.1 V.

M

Shikoku Metan

The cost: it only integrates properly well above about 1.6 Hz.

M

Shikoku Metan

There are also output current limits and slew rate limits. Check the datasheet.

M

Shikoku Metan

Unit 5 recap: internal gain, bandwidth, output range and offset set accuracy and range.
